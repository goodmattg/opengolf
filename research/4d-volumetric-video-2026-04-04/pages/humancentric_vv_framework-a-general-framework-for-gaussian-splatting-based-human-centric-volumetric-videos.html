Abstract

Volumetric video is revolutionizing immersive media, with 3D Gaussian Splatting emerging as a key technology due to its unprecedented real-time rendering quality. However, extending this technology to dynamic scenes presents two major challenges: the massive storage and transmission overhead associated with temporal sequences, and a fragmented toolchain ecosystem that hinders efficient research and development. Existing solutions typically focus on isolated stages such as reconstruction or compression, lacking a unified, end-to-end workflow from data acquisition to final viewing. To address these challenges, we propose a comprehensive dynamic Gaussian processing framework that provides a complete, end-to-end pipeline. This framework systematically integrates the entire process, from data acquisition and standardized preprocessing to a suite of diverse dynamic Gaussian reconstruction algorithms. One of its core contributions is a general-purpose compression framework, compatible with the outputs of various reconstruction methods, which significantly reduces the storage footprint of dynamic sequences while maintaining high visual fidelity. To ensure broad accessibility, we have also developed a cross-platform rendering solution that supports high-quality, interactive free-viewpoint experiences on desktop, mobile, and XR devices. Furthermore, to advance the field, we contribute a large-scale, high-quality dynamic human performance capture dataset. Captured with a dense 81-camera array, the dataset comprises over 130 sequences of diverse human motions, including complex interactions with topological changes. Our integrated framework and dataset aim to bridge the entire pipeline from data creation to end-user application, providing a solid foundation for the large-scale adoption and future research of Gaussian Splatting technology.

1 Introduction

Volumetric video fundamentally reshapes the landscape of visual media. It enables free-viewpoint exploration of immersive virtual environments, effectively narrowing the gap between digital and physical realities. In this context, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] has emerged as a prominent representation method in the fields of 3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis due to its exceptional rendering quality and real-time capabilities. In recent years, researchers have extended 3DGS to dynamic scenes, proposing various 4DGS representations that enable high-fidelity rendering from arbitrary viewpoints and timestamps, providing strong support for applications such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and next-generation immersive media.

However, despite the outstanding visual quality and rendering efficiency of 3DGS and 4DGS, their practical adoption faces significant challenges. The most pressing issue is the high storage cost, especially for dynamic scenes where the introduction of the temporal dimension dramatically increases the data volume, imposing greater demands on storage, transmission, and real-time interaction. On the other hand, although numerous studies have been devoted to the compression and optimization of Gaussian Splatting, these efforts are often scattered across different codebases, plagued by inconsistent data formats and incompatible dataloaders, which hinder the reproduction, comparison, and integration of various methods. Most existing solutions focus only on isolated aspects such as reconstruction or compression, lacking a complete pipeline that covers data acquisition, unified formatting, multi-model reconstruction, general-purpose compression, and cross-platform rendering.

To systematically advance the field and promote practical applications, this paper presents an integrated and modular Gaussian Splatting processing platform that supports end-to-end processing from data acquisition to multi-platform rendering. Our platform incorporates a variety of mainstream and cutting-edge 3DGS and 4DGS reconstruction methods, provides standardized data preprocessing interfaces and unified data loading mechanisms, and includes a general compression framework adaptable to multiple representations. Additionally, we have developed a cross-platform real-time rendering plugin that enables high-quality, interactive free-viewpoint experiences on desktop, mobile, and XR devices.

Finally, we contribute a large-scale, high-quality dynamic human motion capture dataset. To capture this dataset, we built a dense multi-view acquisition system containing 81 synchronized RGB cameras. The system is capable of recording timecode-aligned 3840 × 2160 resolution video at a frame rate of 30 FPS. Based on this acquisition setup, we constructed a dataset containing more than 130 video sequences. The dataset covers more than 20 performers in different costumes with a wide range of movements, from simple body movements such as walking and speaking, to human interaction scenarios involving complex topological changes.

The main contributions of this platform are as follows:

  1. 1)

    We propose and implement an end-to-end Gaussian Splatting processing platform encompassing data acquisition, format standardization, integration of multiple reconstruction methods, general compression, and multi-platform rendering.

  2. 2)

    We design a compression framework compatible with outputs from different reconstruction methods, significantly reducing the storage overhead of both static and dynamic Gaussian Splatting while maintaining visual quality.

  3. 3)

    We develop a cross-platform real-time rendering tool that supports interactive free-viewpoint exploration on various end-user devices, enhancing practicality and user experience.

  4. 4)

    We contribute a large-scale, human-centered action performance dataset that provides a rich and diverse database for generalizable human action reconstruction studies.

With this platform, we aim to establish a complete pipeline from data acquisition to practical application, promoting the large-scale adoption of Gaussian Splatting technologies in real-world scenarios and providing a reliable and efficient experimental foundation for future research.

2 Related work

2.1 Reconstruction methods

This section traces the technological progression of dynamic reconstruction, moving from traditional explicit geometric representations to modern neural representations. Each paradigm is examined in detail to highlight its foundational principles, key innovations, and inherent limitations.

Traditional non-rigid reconstruction Early approaches to human performance capture predominantly relied on mesh-based representations and non-rigid registration techniques. The pioneering work DynamicFusion [2] introduced real-time non-rigid tracking by leveraging GPU solvers without an explicit template prior. Subsequent methods enhanced robustness and handling of complex scenarios: VolumeDeform [3] combined depth correspondences with SIFT features to reduce drift; Fusion4D [4] and Motion2Fusion [5] employed key-frame strategies to manage topological changes; KillingFusion [6] and SobolevFusion [7] introduced motion field constraints; DoubleFusion [8] and UnstructuredFusion [9] utilized a two-layer representation aided by a human parametric model (e.g., SMPL [10]) for better robustness in unstructured setups; RobustFusion [11, 12] addressed challenging human-object interactions. Methods such as DDC [13] used skeleton-driven deformation graphs [14], while HighQualityFVV [15] relied on tracked mesh sequences with texture video. NeuralHOFusion [16] enables high-fidelity human-object capture and rendering from sparse RGBD sensors via layerwise disentanglement and neural implicit fusion. However, these mesh-based methods often prioritize geometric accuracy and tracking robustness, frequently at the expense of high-frequency detail and texture fidelity.

NeRF-based reconstruction The advent of neural radiance fields (NeRF) [17] revolutionized dynamic scene modeling by bypassing explicit geometry in favor of volumetric neural representations. Early dynamic NeRF variants [18–20] learned deformations between a canonical space and observed frames using photometric differences. Subsequent works integrated stronger inductive biases and efficient representations: many methods [21–26] built upon the SNARF [27] framework to learn skinning weights via root-finding; HumanNeRFs [28, 29] used SMPL as a motion anchor to warp the radiance field; Refs. [30–36] employed 4D feature grids or decomposed tensor volumes to jointly encode spatio-temporal variations. Advances such as hash encoding [37] and tensor decomposition [38] significantly accelerated training and rendering, leading to methods such as Instant-NSR [39], HumanRF [32], and NerfPlayer [40]. Instant-NVR [41] achieves real-time radiance field reconstruction and novel-view synthesis from monocular RGBD input via a multi-thread tracking-rendering architecture and hybrid deformation module. Despite improved realism, NeRF-based methods often struggle with rendering speed, blurry outputs on high-frequency details, and handling fast motions.

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) based reconstruction The introduction of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] marked a shift back to explicit, differentiable point-based representations, enabling real-time, high-fidelity rendering. This sparked rapid development in dynamic 3DGS variants. Some methods model temporal deformation directly: Spacetime Gaussians [42] use polynomial trajectories; Deformable 3DGS [43] and 4DGS [44] employ MLPs to predict per-Gaussian motion. Other methods leverage explicit human priors: GaussianAvatars [45, 46] bind Gaussians to a FLAME face model; D3GA [47] uses tetrahedral cages for deformation control; HiFi4G [48] utilizes embedded deformation graphs [14] to accelerate training. BEAM [49] introduces a relightable 4D Gaussian representation that bridges neural capture with PBR pipelines, enabling high-quality volumetric video relighting from multi-view RGB inputs. A key challenge is handling topological changes. Keyframe-based strategies [4, 48, 50] segment the sequence to mitigate drift, while unified 4D encoding approaches [42, 51, 52] model spacetime in a single structure. Recent methods like GauSTAR [53] introduce explicit surface remeshing for topology changes but face issues with over-smoothing and high computational cost. While excelling in rendering speed and quality, most 3DGS methods are designed for the playback of captured sequences and lack generalization to novel animations.

Frameworks for 3D Gaussian Splatting In recent years, 3D Gaussian Splatting technology has given rise to a series of open-source frameworks. Among them, Gsplat [54], as a foundational library containing efficient CUDA rasterization kernels, along with the modular platform GauStudio [55] built upon it, have greatly advanced the development of static scene reconstruction. For dynamic scenarios, particularly human performance capture, EasyVolCap [56] provides a flexible, research-oriented platform for benchmarking and evaluating various dynamic volumetric capture algorithms. However, the core focus of these existing frameworks is primarily on the reconstruction algorithms themselves, and they offer relatively limited native support for complete end-to-end pipeline components, such as standardized data preprocessing, post-reconstruction sequence compression, and cross-platform rendering deployment.

2.2 Data compression

The increasing fidelity and detail of 4D reconstruction methods have created a parallel challenge: the need for efficient and compact data representation for storage and transmission.

Traditional mesh and point cloud compression Before the advent of neural representations, traditional methods focused on compressing mesh and point cloud data. For meshes, techniques aimed at reducing geometric redundancy through PCA-based compression [57, 58] and mesh segmentation [59] were common. To maintain temporal coherence in animated meshes, vertex trajectory prediction was also used [58, 60]. For point clouds, the field was formalized by standards like MPEG-PCC, which includes video-based (VPCC) and geometry-based (GPCC) compression. These standards rely on classic data structures such as octrees [61] and k-d trees, along with wavelet encoding [62], with subsequent improvements from learning-based methods [63, 64].

Compression of neural fields In the era of neural fields, compression is not about geometric primitives but about designing compact network architectures or representations. For static scenes, compactness is achieved through tensor decomposition [38], tri-plane factorization [65, 66], and low-rank approximation [67]. These methods effectively reduce the number of parameters needed to represent the 3D scene. For dynamic scenes, these ideas are extended to the spatio-temporal domain. For example, 4K4D [51] uses a 4D feature grid, while VideoRF [68] encodes the 4D radiance field as a 2D feature stream. HumanRF [32] employs temporal matrix-vector decomposition, and ReRF [69] uses a residual radiance field to reduce data redundancy between frames. HPC [70] and VRVVC [71] introduce variable-rate coding frameworks, enabling flexible control over the rate-distortion trade-off within single, unified models.

Compression of 3D Gaussian Splatting The transition to 3DGS presents a fundamentally new compression problem. Unlike with NeRFs, where the goal is to compress network weights, the challenge here is to compress the millions of attribute-rich, explicit Gaussians (position, rotation, scale, color and opacity) that compose the scene. This new problem has led to novel solutions that draw from both traditional point cloud compression and modern codec techniques.

Common strategies for static scenes include vector quantization and entropy encoding, as implemented in Compact3D [72], C3DGS [73], and Compact-3DGS [74]. Other methods focus on sparsification by pruning redundant Gaussians, as seen in LightGaussian [75], or by using anchor-based approaches to model the scene more efficiently, such as Scaffold-GS [76] and HAC [77]. A particularly clever approach is to map the Gaussian attributes onto 2D grids and compress them using standard image or video codecs, as seen in SOG [78]. For dynamic Gaussians, methods such as HiFi4G [48] and V3 [50] apply residual computation and entropy encoding within a keyframe setting to exploit temporal coherence. 4DGC [79] exploits inter-frame similarities by utilizing a compact motion grid in combination with sparse compensated Gaussians. To further enhance streaming flexibility, 4DGCPro [80] proposes a hierarchical 4D Gaussian framework with motion-aware grouping, enabling progressive transmission and real-time mobile decoding. From a delivery system perspective, VARFVV [81] introduces an edge-assisted reassembly scheme with popularity-adaptive bit allocation to optimize bandwidth usage and QoE for immersive video. A major trade-off remains, as these approaches often struggle to achieve a high compression ratio without compromising rendering quality. Additionally, dynamic methods that rely on keyframes face the issue of costly resets to handle significant topological changes, which can impact streaming performance.

3 Preliminary

3D Gaussian Splatting [1] is a scene representation and rendering method based on explicit primitives, the core of which is to model real-world scenes using a set of 3D Gaussian ellipsoids. Through an efficient rasterization pipeline and a fully differentiable rendering process, this method can perform high-quality novel view synthesis for complex scenes and achieve real-time rendering frame rates. During the optimization process, the system employs stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to iteratively adjust the attribute parameters of each Gaussian ellipsoid until the difference between the rendered result and the ground-truth images is minimized, thereby accurately reproducing the scene’s appearance.

In this representation, each 3D Gaussian ellipsoid is defined by a set of optimizable attribute parameters, primarily including: position \(\mu _{i}\), covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\varSigma} _{i}\), opacity \(\sigma_{i}\), and spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients \(h_{i}\).

The geometric form of a Gaussian ellipsoid in 3D space is jointly determined by its center point μ and its covariance matrix Σ. For any point x in space, its probability density relative to this Gaussian distribution can be calculated by the following multivariate Gaussian function:

$$ G(X) = \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}X^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\varSigma} ^{-1}X}. $$
(1)

In the optimization process, the covariance matrix Σ must strictly adhere to the mathematical constraint of being positive semi-definite. To maintain this property throughout the optimization and to simplify the problem, the covariance matrix is not optimized directly. Instead, it is parameterized by a rotation matrix R and a scaling matrix S. This decomposition is formulated as follows:

$$ \boldsymbol{\varSigma} = \boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{S} \boldsymbol{S}^{\mathrm{T}} \boldsymbol{R}^{\mathrm{T}}, $$
(2)

where S is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements define the scaling factors of the Gaussian ellipsoid along the three principal axes of its local coordinate system. R is an orthonormal rotation matrix that determines the pose or orientation of the ellipsoid in 3D space. In practice, to avoid gimbal lock and ensure numerical stability during optimization, the rotation matrix R is typically parameterized by a quaternion \(q \in \mathbb{R}^{4}\).

For any given camera view, the rendering process can be decomposed into two main stages: projection from 3D to 2D, and depth-based alpha blending. In the projection stage, all 3D Gaussians in the scene are projected onto the 2D image plane. This is typically achieved using a standard viewing transformation matrix W. The core of a 3D Gaussian is its covariance matrix Σ, which is approximated into a 2D covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\varSigma}'\) in camera coordinates via the Jacobian J of the affine transformation. This is computed as follows:

$$ \boldsymbol{\varSigma} = \boldsymbol{J} \boldsymbol{W} \boldsymbol{\varSigma} \boldsymbol{W}^{\mathrm{T}} \boldsymbol{J}^{ \mathrm{T}}. $$
(3)

This step is geometrically equivalent to projecting and transforming the 3D Gaussian ellipsoids into 2D ellipses in image space, which provides the necessary 2D primitives for the subsequent rasterization stage.

In the alpha blending stage, after all Gaussians have been projected onto the 2D plane, the system sorts them in a front-to-back order based on their depth values. Subsequently, the final color C for each pixel is calculated using the classic alpha blending algorithm. This algorithm accumulates the color and opacity contributions from all the sorted 2D Gaussians that overlap the pixel, as formulated below:

$$ C = \sum _{i \in N} c_{i} \alpha _{i} \prod _{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha _{j}), $$
(4)

where \(c_{i}\) is the color of the i-th Gaussian primitive, calculated from the Spherical Harmonics (SH) model based on the current viewing direction. \(\alpha _{i}\) is the corresponding opacity of that primitive. The core component is the product term, \(\prod _{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha _{j})\), which precisely calculates the accumulated transmittance of all preceding Gaussians (i.e., those with a sort index less than i), thereby ensuring the correct representation of occlusion relationships in the scene.

4 Framework overview

We present a comprehensive, end-to-end framework that systematically addresses the entire lifecycle management of dynamic Gaussian sequences, encompassing data preprocessing, dynamic scene reconstruction, efficient compression, and final cross-platform rendering. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this framework consists of four core modules: 1) data preprocessing, 2) dynamic reconstruction, 3) sequence compression, and 4) a multi-platform renderer. The framework is designed to provide researchers and developers with a standardized workflow to efficiently process custom multi-view video data and generate deployable dynamic Gaussian representations.

The data preprocessing module offers a rigorous workflow to convert raw, time-code-aligned multi-view videos into a standardized dataset suitable for dynamic Gaussian reconstruction. This workflow includes three critical steps: camera parameter calibration, scene space normalization, and foreground matting to isolate the dynamic subject.

In the dynamic reconstruction stage, to accommodate dynamic scenes of varying complexity, our framework integrates a suite of three distinct reconstruction algorithms. These algorithms cover the spectrum from rapid reconstruction optimized for efficiency to complex dynamic scene reconstruction capable of accurately capturing topological changes. This design grants users the flexibility to make a trade-off between computational cost and reconstruction fidelity based on the dynamic characteristics of the source data and to select the optimal reconstruction strategy.

For sequence compression, the framework introduces a novel compression scheme specifically designed for sequences composed of per-frame, independent Gaussian sets. It encodes standard 3D Gaussians into a binary stream via codebook quantization and entropy encoding. Our compression strategy achieves a high compression ratio with extremely low loss, and its lightweight decoding algorithm ensures real-time decompression performance on mainstream consumer devices.

Finally, to close the loop from content creation to consumption, we have developed bespoke real-time renderers compatible with our compression format. These renderers have been implemented on Unity, IOS devices, and web browser platforms. This broad platform support ensures that our dynamic Gaussian content can be widely deployed and accessed across diverse application scenarios.

5.1 Dynamic Gaussian modeling

For the training stage, we adopt a frame-independent training strategy. This strategy models the dynamic sequence chronologically as a series of frame-independent Gaussian point cloud sets, effectively forming a representation structurally analogous to a video. This representation not only greatly simplifies the compression of dynamic Gaussians but also facilitates random access to frames within the sequence. To accommodate dynamic content of varying complexity, our framework integrates three advanced dynamic modeling algorithms: DualGS [82], TaoGS, and RePerformer [83].

5.1.1 DualGS

DualGS [82] introduces a hierarchical, dual-Gaussian representation to model dynamic human performances, separating motion and appearance to enhance temporal coherence. Its core represents a dynamic human with two coupled sets of Gaussians: a small number of joint Gaussians that capture broad, underlying motions and a much larger set of skin Gaussians that renders fine-grained appearance details. This decoupled structure is optimized through a coarse-to-fine, frame-by-frame training process.

Dual-Gaussian representation The system first initializes a sparse set of approximately 15,000 joint Gaussians from a random point cloud. These are optimized to capture the global motion and overall shape of the performer. To prevent them from becoming overly elongated or large during this initial optimization, two constraints are applied. The complete loss function for optimizing the joint Gaussians is:

$$\begin{aligned}& \begin{aligned} E_{\mathrm{iso}} & =\frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \text{ReLU}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{max}(s_{i}) - \mathrm{min}(s_{i})} - r), \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(5)
$$\begin{aligned}& \begin{aligned} E_{\mathrm{size}}=\sum _{i=1}^{N} \text{ReLU} \left (s_{i}- \alpha \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} sg[s_{i}] \right ), \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(6)
$$\begin{aligned}& E_{\mathrm{init}} = \lambda _{\mathrm{iso}} E_{\mathrm{iso}} + \lambda _{\mathrm{size}} E_{\mathrm{size}} + E_{\mathrm {color}}, \end{aligned}$$
(7)

where \(E_{\mathrm{iso}}\) is an isotropic loss that constrains overly skinny scaling. \(E_{\text{size}}\) is used to constrain oversized Gaussians, preventing local over-reconstruction. \(E_{\text{color}}\) is the photometric loss.

Once the joint Gaussians are optimized, a dense set of approximately 180,000 skin Gaussians is initialized to capture the performer’s detailed appearance (color, opacity, and scale). Crucially, each skin Gaussian is anchored to its k-nearest neighboring joint Gaussians (k=8). The influence of each joint Gaussian \(p_{k}^{j}\) on a skin Gaussian \(p_{i}^{s}\) is determined by a blending weight w, calculated using Eq. (8):

$$ w(p_{i}^{s}, p_{k}^{j}) = \exp (-||p_{i}^{s} - p_{k}^{j}||_{2}^{2} / l^{2}), $$
(8)

where l is the influence radius. This establishes a fixed relationship that allows the motion of the skin Gaussians to be interpolated from their corresponding joints.

Coarse-to-Fine optimization For subsequent frames, DualGS employs a novel coarse-to-fine optimization strategy to ensure robust tracking and high-fidelity rendering.

In the first phase, only the motion parameters (position and rotation) of the sparse joint Gaussians are optimized. To ensure physical plausibility and smooth motion, a local as-rigid-as-possible (ARAP) regularizer is applied:

$$ \begin{aligned} E_{\text{smooth}}= & \sum _{i} \sum _{k \in \mathcal{N}(i)} w_{i, k} \| R\left (q^{j}_{i, t} * {q^{j}_{i, t-1}}^{-1}\right ) \\ & \left (p^{j}_{k, t-1}-p^{j}_{i, t-1}\right )-\left (p^{j}_{k, t}-p^{j}_{i, t}\right ) \|_{2}^{2}, \end{aligned} $$
(9)

where \(w_{i, k}\) denotes the weight representing the connection strength between the i-th Gaussian and its neighbor k, while \(p^{j}_{k, t}\) and \(q^{j}_{q, t}\) represent the position and rotation quaternion at time step t, respectively. In the second phase, the optimized motions of the joint Gaussians are used to interpolate the initial positions \(p_{i,t}^{s}\) and rotations \(q_{i,t}^{s}\) of the dense skin Gaussians for the current frame:

$$\begin{aligned}& p_{i,t}^{s} = \sum _{k \in \mathcal{N}(p_{i,1}^{s})} w(p_{i,1}^{s}, p_{k,1}^{j}) (R(q_{k,t}^{j}) p_{i,1}^{s} + p_{k,t}^{j}), \end{aligned}$$
(10)
$$\begin{aligned}& q_{i,t}^{s} = \sum _{k \in \mathcal{N}(p_{i,1}^{s})} w(p_{i,1}^{s}, p_{k,1}^{j}) q_{k,t}^{j}. \end{aligned}$$
(11)

A fine-grained optimization is then performed to refine the detailed positions and appearance attributes of the skin Gaussians. Temporal regularizers are applied to these attributes to prevent significant changes between consecutive frames, enhancing temporal consistency:

$$ E_{\text{temp}} = \sum _{a\in \{C,\sigma ,s\}}\lambda _{a}||a_{i,t}-a_{i,t-1}||_{2}^{2}. $$
(12)

This two-stage process effectively disentangles motion and appearance, significantly improving tracking accuracy and rendering quality.

5.1.2 TaoGS

TaoGS [84] introduces a novel topology-aware dynamic Gaussian representation designed to tackle the challenging problem of modeling dynamic scenes, especially those undergoing topological changes. Its core innovation lies in a decoupled motion-appearance modeling strategy, which enables robust long-range tracking, high-fidelity rendering, and efficient training. The method operates through a sequential process of mesh-free initialization, motion tracking, topological adaptation, and appearance synthesis.

Motion Gaussians The foundation of TaoGS is a sparse set of motion Gaussians, which form a deformation graph to capture the underlying non-rigid dynamics of the scene. The process begins with a mesh-free initialization. Utilizing dense 2D correspondences from a pre-trained matcher and triangulation, the method generates an initial point cloud to bootstrap a sparse set of Gaussians, effectively capturing fine details such as hair and edges that are often missed by traditional SfM [85] or mesh-based methods. These motion Gaussians are optimized under a powerful ARAP regularizer as demonstrated on Eq. (9) applied to their deformation graph, ensuring that their motion is physically plausible and serves as a stable anchor for the entire representation. The ARAP constraint is formalized as a smoothness loss \(E_{\text{smooth}}\) on the deformation graph, regularizing both position p and rotation q across frames. This loss is combined with a photometric rendering loss \(E_{\text{color}}\) for joint optimization:

$$ E=\lambda _{\text{smooth}}E_{\text{smooth}}+E_{\text{color}}. $$
(13)

Appearance Gaussians To achieve high visual fidelity, each motion Gaussian acts as an anchor, deriving and dynamically activating a set of appearance Gaussians. These appearance Gaussians are responsible for modeling the scene’s fine-grained textures. Specifically, each motion Gaussian is split into K appearance Gaussians (K=9 in our method). These appearance Gaussians remain consistently linked to their corresponding anchor throughout the subsequent warping and training process. At the beginning of each frame’s training, appearance Gaussians are non-rigidly warped to the current frame by their anchor motion Gaussian, following a warping strategy similar to that used in DualGS. This provides a strong initialization, allowing them to converge to a high-quality rendering with minimal fine-tuning. The key advantage of this decoupled design is that it isolates the challenges of motion tracking and detail synthesis, drastically reducing redundancy and accelerating training. A motion-to-appearance graph enforces temporal coherence among these activated Gaussians, ensuring stable rendering even during rapid motion. The initial set of Gaussians from which motion Gaussians are derived is optimized using a joint loss function that promotes a spatially regular distribution suitable for surface approximation:

$$ E_{\text{init}}=\lambda _{\text{lap}}E_{\text{lap}}+\lambda _{\text{iso}}E_{ \text{iso}}+\lambda _{\text{size}}E_{\text{size}}+E_{\text{color}}, $$
(14)

where \(E_{\text{lap}} \) denotes the Laplacian smoothness loss, which enforces local geometric consistency and prevents overlapping regions, yielding a spatially coherent distribution that approximates the surface’s zero-level set and stabilizes the following optimization:

$$ \begin{aligned} E_{\text{lap}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \left \| p_{i,1} - sg\left [ \frac{1}{K} \sum _{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} (p_{j,1}) \right ] \right \|^{2}. \end{aligned} $$
(15)

For isotropic loss \(E_{\text{iso}} \) and size loss \(E_{\text{size}} \), please refer to Eq. (5) and Eq. (6).

Topological adaptation The mechanism for handling topological changes is deeply integrated into the motion Gaussian pipeline. A temporal tracker (e.g., CoTracker) estimates per-pixel optical flow and a binary emergence mask \(\mathcal{M}_{t \rightarrow t-1}^{v}\) between consecutive frames, where a value of 0 indicates a newly observed pixel. This process is formalized as

$$ \mathcal{T}(I_{t}^{v},I_{t-1}^{v})\rightarrow \{\mathcal{W}_{t\to t-1}^{v}, \mathcal{M}_{t\to t-1}^{v}\}, $$
(16)

where \(\mathcal{W}\) is a dense warp field and \(\mathcal{M}\) is the binary mask. Candidate Gaussians \(\mathcal{G}_{\text{cand}}\), generated from dense spatial matching across paired views, are first filtered using this mask: a candidate is retained only if its corresponding pixels in both views are marked as new. This first-stage filtering weight \(w_{1}\) is defined as

$$ w_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{cand}}^{k})=(1-\mathcal{M}_{t\to t-1}^{v}(u_{1})) \times (1-\mathcal{M}_{t\to t-1}^{v}(u_{2})), $$
(17)

where \(u_{1}\) and \(u_{2}\) are the corresponding pixels in the paired views \(v_{1}\) and \(v_{2}\). A candidate is kept if \(w_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{cand}}^{k})=1\).

However, the temporal mask alone cannot distinguish true topological changes from occlusions. To suppress these false positives, a second filtering stage employs photometric error: candidates projecting into poorly reconstructed image regions (with high \(E_{\text{color}}\)) are kept, while those in well-explained regions are discarded via a threshold ϵ. This second-stage filtering uses a confidence score based on the photometric error at the projected pixels:

$$ w_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{cand}}^{k})=E_{\text{color}}^{v_{1}}(u_{1})+E_{ \text{color}}^{v_{2}}(u_{2}), $$
(18)

where candidates with a score \(w_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{cand}})\) below a threshold ϵ are discarded. This two-stage process ensures that only Gaussians representing genuine new observations are integrated into the deformation graph, where the ARAP constraint ensures their smooth incorporation. This continuous detection-filtering-integration cycle enables robust adaptation to topological changes such as clothing changes or object interactions.

Unified workflow and efficiency The synergy between these components creates a highly efficient and effective workflow. The sparse set of motion Gaussians provides a compact and stable foundation for tracking, enabling rapid optimization convergence. The activation of appearance Gaussians from this foundation ensures that detailed rendering does not come at the cost of excessive training time or storage. By unifying mesh-free initialization, spatio-temporal topological adaptation, and a decoupled motion-appearance architecture, TaoGS achieves robust performance capture and high-fidelity novel-view synthesis under complex topological variations.

5.1.3 Reperformer

RePerformer [83] builds directly upon the hierarchical representation introduced in DualGS, which disentangles a dynamic scene into sparse motion Gaussians for dynamics and dense appearance Gaussians for detail. RePerformer adopts this core concept of using an underlying motion structure to drive the detailed appearance representation. However, it introduces a significant departure from DualGS’s per-frame optimization strategy by creating a generalizable model that learns to render appearance from motion. This is achieved through a novel 3D-to-2D parameterization of the appearance Gaussians and a subsequent neural training stage.

3D-to-2D mapping While inheriting the concept of motion-driven appearance Gaussians, RePerformer parameterizes them into a 2D format suitable for CNNs. A Morton-based parameterization is used to project the 3D positions of the canonical appearance Gaussians onto a 2D plane. This process sorts the Gaussians by interleaving the binary representations of their spatial coordinates, which preserves 3D spatial adjacency in the resulting 2D map. This creates a consistent mapping from each Gaussian’s index i to a UV coordinate (u,v). Using this mapping, the warped 3D positions of the appearance Gaussians for any frame can be organized into a 2D position map.

2D CNN training Instead of overfitting appearance Gaussians for each frame via gradient descent as in DualGS, RePerformer leverages 2D convolutional architectures to regress Gaussian attributes from the 2D position map, which enhances generalization. Three separate U-Net architectures are adopted to learn the mapping to motion-aware attribute maps: a Rotation Net, a Shape Net (for opacity and scaling), and a Color Net (for SH parameters). To address the fact that Morton ordering only partially preserves global spatial consistency, a self-attention layer is deployed before the final U-Net upsampling step to improve generalization.

The training process is divided into two stages. First, a pre-training warm-up stage supervises the U-Nets using the warped appearance Gaussians as pseudo ground truth, optimizing an \(\mathcal{L}_{2}\) loss below:

$$ E_{\text{pre}}=\mathcal{L}_{2}\left (\mathcal{G},\mathcal{G}^{\prime} \right ) , $$
(19)

where \(\mathcal{G}\) denotes the Gaussians being optimized (predicted by the U-Nets), and \(\mathcal{G}'\) represents the warped appearance Gaussians that act as the pseudo ground truth. In the main training stage, the attribute maps produced by the U-Nets are assembled back into Gaussians for rasterization, and the networks are supervised directly with an \(\mathcal{L}_{1}\) photometric loss and a D-SSIM term:

$$ E_{\text{train}}=\left (1-\lambda _{\text{color}}\right ) \mathcal{L}_{1}+\lambda _{\text{color}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}}. $$
(20)

5.2 Compression

To further compress the reconstructed Gaussian point cloud sequences, we propose a compression strategy that combines codebook quantization and entropy encoding. This strategy can process any Gaussian sequence represented on a per-frame basis. Although it achieves optimal performance on sequences generated by DualGS or RePerformer, which possess good spatio-temporal consistency, it is also compatible with point cloud sequences generated by other algorithms that may lack explicit spatial tracking. Our method achieves a compression ratio of up to 36x while ensuring minimal quality loss. Figure 3 illustrates the complete workflow of the compression module.

Preprocess For point cloud data not generated by DualGS or RePerformer, we perform a pre-sorting step to ensure that point clouds with the same index are spatially close throughout the model during the subsequent compression process. We use Morton Order to sort the positions of the point cloud. In detail, for an unordered 3D Gaussian point cloud, we first convert the coordinates of each point from float to integers with a range of 221, and then calculate their Morton codes and sort them. This is done to make the residual matrix in the subsequent residual process as sparse as possible.

Dual-codebook quantization Inspired by static Gaussian compression methods and the DualGS framework, we first segment the point cloud sequence into blocks of 30 frames. Subsequently, we partition the Gaussian attributes into 15 independent clusters: x, y, and z are clustered independently; opacity and scaling factors are combined into one cluster; rotation is treated as one cluster; the 0-th order RGB is combined into one cluster; and the higher-order spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients are divided into \(3 \times 3 = 9\) clusters based on their order and color channel.

For each attribute cluster, we perform k-Means clustering twice (with k=128 kernels) to generate two codebooks. The codebooks are initialized with a uniform distribution, iteratively refined using randomly sampled training batches, and are also sorted to ensure that adjacent indices correspond to similar attribute values. The process is as follows. First, we aggregate all point cloud data within a single block (30 frames). We then perform the first k-Means clustering on this aggregated data to obtain the first codebook, \(C_{1}\), and the corresponding index for each point, \(I_{1}\). Next, we reconstruct the attribute values using \(C_{1}\) and \(I_{1}\) and calculate the residual by subtracting the reconstructed values from the original ones. The second k-Means clustering is performed on these residuals to generate the second codebook, \(C_{2}\), and the residual index, \(I_{2}\). The reconstructed attribute Â can be calculated using Eq. (21):

$$ \hat{A} = C_{1}[I_{1}] + C_{2}[I_{2}]. $$
(21)

Ultimately, for each block, we obtain \(2 \times 15\) index maps, where each index value can be stored using only 7 bits. The corresponding codebooks are stored separately in float32 format. This dual-codebook quantization strategy efficiently encodes the distribution of point cloud attributes with low error, thereby significantly reducing storage volume.

Index residual encoding Although codebook quantization effectively eliminates intra-frame redundancy, it does not address the temporal redundancy between adjacent frames in a dynamic sequence. In most dynamic sequences, the attributes of the majority of point clouds change minimally over time. Therefore, we can eliminate temporal redundancy by calculating the difference between the index maps of consecutive frames.

For each block, we only store the complete index map of the first frame, \(I_{f}\). For each subsequent frame, \(f+1\), we only store its change relative to the preceding frame, \(\Delta I_{f+1}\). If the number of point clouds in the current frame is inconsistent with the previous frame, the absolute values are retained for the portion that exceeds the length of the previous frame:

$$ \Delta I_{f+1} = I_{f+1} - I_{f}. $$
(22)

By calculating these residuals, the original sequence of index maps is transformed into a sequence of sparse matrices. Since the codebook indices range from [0, 127], the residual values are strictly bounded within the range [-127, 127], allowing them to be efficiently stored using a signed 8-bit integer (int8) type.

Entropy encoding The calculated index residuals form a sparse vector with a zero-centered data distribution. To further eliminate the spatial redundancy within this vector, we leverage its distributional characteristics by encoding it using the ranged arithmetic numerical system (RANS). RANS is an entropy coding method that compresses data by calculating the frequency of each quantized value and constructing a probability distribution model. The algorithm updates a state value based on each input symbol (i.e., the index value) and the encoder’s current state, where the final state represents the encoded data sequence.

For our sparse index residuals, RANS can efficiently handle the high prevalence of zero or near-zero values. Specifically, we reconstruct the residual index vector into a point cloud format, where each point contains 27 attributes of type int8. The XYZ coordinates are composed of the indices obtained from the first-stage codebook quantization of the original position (x, y, z) attributes.

Subsequently, we use the Draco geometric compression library to process it into a binary stream. Under its lossless compression mode, Draco can achieve an additional compression of approximately 4.5× on the RANS-encoded index sequence, bringing the total compression ratio to 36×. Based on this two-stage compression strategy, we can compress the massive raw Gaussian data to a bitrate compatible with normal network bandwidths. This enables real-time streaming over the Internet, allowing users to immersively watch various dynamic Gaussian sequence performances online.

6 Datasets

In dynamic Gaussian reconstruction, the quality and view density of the input data are critical factors that determine the final reconstruction fidelity. High-quality and view-dense video inputs significantly enhance the fidelity of the reconstructed Gaussian model and ensure its geometric and appearance completeness under any novel view. In this section, we will first detail the hardware system used for data acquisition, then present a high-quality and diverse dataset of human interaction and performance captured with this system, and finally, we will describe our standardized data preprocessing pipeline. Figure 5 shows selected examples from our datasets.

6.1 Human performance dataset

Our data acquisition system consists of a dense multi-view array of 81 high-resolution RGB cameras. All cameras are uniformly mounted on a cylindrical frame with a radius of 5 meters and are precisely postured to ensure that the complete surface of a performer can be covered without any dead angle. In order to capture both global dynamics and localized details, approximately one-third of the cameras were set to a zoomed-in focal length, specifically designed to capture high-fidelity, detailed textures, such as facial expressions and costume details. The entire camera array is tightly synchronized to record multi-view video at 4K resolution (3840 × 2160) with timecode alignment at 30 FPS. The system provides a circular performance area with a radius of 1.5 meters in the center of the venue, allowing the captured individuals to freely perform various types of dynamic performances within the space.

Leveraging the aforementioned acquisition system, we have constructed a large-scale and diverse dataset for dynamic human performance. The dataset comprises 130 distinct video sequences with a total duration of 143 minutes, featuring over 20 different performers in 58 unique outfits. The total volume of the data is substantial, consisting of approximately 33.4 million images at 4K resolution.

To accommodate various research needs, the sequence lengths within the dataset range from short 3-second clips to extended takes of over 8 minutes (500 seconds). In terms of motion design, we have comprehensively covered a spectrum of scenarios from simple to complex. The dataset includes not only fundamental body movements such as walking and dialogue but also intricate multi-person interaction scenarios involving significant topological changes. This diverse content distribution provides a multi-level benchmark with varying degrees of difficulty for dynamic reconstruction tasks.

6.2 Data preprocess

To convert raw videos captured directly by cameras into a format suitable for training our dynamic Gaussian Splatting models, we designed and implemented a comprehensive data preprocessing pipeline. This pipeline ensures that all input data possess accurate camera calibration parameters, a unified coordinate system, and clean foreground objects, all of which are crucial for high-quality dynamic 3D scene reconstruction. Our workflow consists of three key stages: camera pose estimation, scene space normalization, and dynamic foreground matting.

Accurate intrinsic and extrinsic camera parameters form the foundation of 3D reconstruction. We employ the industry-standard structure-from-motion (SfM) tool, COLMAP [85], to process the sequence of images extracted from the video. COLMAP robustly analyzes correspondences between images, jointly optimizing and estimating a sparse 3D point cloud along with precise camera intrinsics (e.g., focal length, principal point) and extrinsics (rotation matrix and translation vector) for each frame. The output of this process provides the essential geometric information required for subsequent scene normalization and model training.

The scene reconstruction obtained through SfM possesses inherent uncertainties in global scale, position, and orientation. Furthermore, stochastic processes within COLMAP can lead to slight coordinate system discrepancies across multiple calibrations of the same scene. To eliminate these ambiguities introduced by both the algorithm and multi-scene data, and to ensure that stable reconstruction results reside within a consistent coordinate system for a given capture setup, we introduce a scene space normalization step. This step transforms the camera parameters and point cloud of each scene into a canonical coordinate space—a cubic volume centered at the origin with boundaries at [-1, 1].

For dynamic scenes, our model focuses on reconstructing the dynamic subject. To eliminate interference from the static background during model training and achieve a clean reconstruction of the foreground dynamics, we incorporate a refined foreground matting stage. Specifically, we utilize the BackgroundMattingV2 algorithm. This algorithm requires the original image sequence and a pre-captured, clean background image devoid of the dynamic subject. By comparing each frame against the background image, BackgroundMattingV2 generates a high-quality alpha matte for each frame. Subsequently, we employ a graph cut algorithm, following the approach used in DNA-Rendering, to further refine the background mask. We use this mask to precisely separate the dynamic subject from the original background. During subsequent training, the corresponding mask is used to control the background color, which is randomly switched among various colors. This strategy helps mitigate edge artifacts often caused by using a solid color background in standard Gaussian training. After this processing, the data fed into the model contain only pristine foreground information.

7 Experiment

7.1 Reconstruction comparison

We compare our methods with the state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods, including Hifi4G [48], V3 [50], Spacetime Gaussian [42], SC-GS [86], 4DGS [87], and NeuS2 [88], on two types of our datasets: those without topological changes and those with topological changes.

As shown in Fig. 6, on the dataset without topological changes, Hifi4G—which heavily relies on explicit mesh reconstruction and non-rigid tracking—generates severely unnatural results with failed deformations. The motion prediction network used in V3 tends to produce noise in regions with fast local motion. Spacetime Gaussian struggles to reconstruct high-frequency details, while SC-GS often produces blur in areas with large motions. 4DGS frequently fails or introduces spiky artifacts, and NeuS2, due to its implicit representation, has difficulty recovering fine textures. In contrast, our three methods (DualGS, Reperformer, and TaoGS) benefit from a decoupled representation of motion and appearance, enabling high-quality reconstruction of fast-moving regions while preserving detailed textures.

On the dataset with topological changes, as illustrated in Fig. 7, both HiFi4G and V3 rely on costly keyframe strategies and suffer from intra-segment distortions. The representations used by Spacetime Gaussian and 4DGS struggle to model rapidly moving regions, resulting in blurred outputs. Due to the smoothness bias of implicit networks, SC-GS and NeuS2 fail to reconstruct fine textures. Among our three methods, both DualGS and Reperformer—which rely on ARAP-based point cloud tracking—produce artifacts in regions with topological changes. In comparison, TaoGS leverages a spatio-temporal tracker to adaptively insert new motion Gaussians and activate associated appearance Gaussians, enabling robust tracking and high-fidelity rendering under topology changes.

For quantitative comparison, we evaluate each method across three sequences on the two types of datasets, respectively, each consisting of 200 frames. In addition to metrics such as PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and training time, the results are summarized in Table 1. Our methods achieve the highest rendering quality on sequences without topological changes. On sequences with topological changes, TaoGS delivers the best results. Each of our three methods has its own strengths and limitations: TaoGS robustly handles topological changes with low computational overhead; DualGS achieves very high quality on data without topological changes, though at a higher computational cost; and Reperformer enables relatively high-quality reconstruction with very low time consumption.

7.2 Compression comparison

We evaluated our proposed compression algorithm on our proprietary dataset and compared it against state-of-the-art dynamic and static compression methods. Unlike approaches such as Spacetime Gaussian [42], which embed compression into the training process, our method employs a post-training compression scheme and therefore does not depend on a specific reconstruction algorithm.

As shown in Table 2, our method surpasses these training-integrated reconstruction algorithms in terms of compression ratio while maintaining the compression loss within an acceptable range. The quantitative results further demonstrate that our method achieves a compression ratio of approximately 36x relative to the original uncompressed Gaussian data, and also outperforms other post-training compression algorithms like V3 [50] in rendering quality. Furthermore, our method integrates efficient decoding strategies for web browsers and mobile devices, achieving real-time decoding speeds of over 50 FPS on resource-constrained hardware.

7.3 Compression ablation

We designed an ablation study to quantitatively evaluate the impact of our codebook and the quantization bit-depth for entropy encoding within our compression algorithm. We randomly selected 30 frames from the DualGS reconstruction results, compressed them under various parameter configurations, and evaluated their rendering metrics post-decompression.

As shown in Table 3, our codebook quantization strategy effectively preserves the majority of key attributes from the original Gaussians while achieving a significant reduction in storage volume. Furthermore, the results indicate that setting Draco’s quantization parameter to an 8-bit precision level, as opposed to a 6-bit setting, yields higher reconstruction quality for an acceptable increase in storage cost.

Furthermore, to investigate the relationship between the dimensionality of attribute clusters and their sensitivity to quantization distortion, we measured the relative percentage error of our dual-codebook quantization against the original attribute values. Our findings show that attributes with lower dimensionality are remarkably resilient to quantization. For instance, the position attribute (3 channels) exhibits a negligible error of only 0.03% ∼ 0.05%. The rotation attribute (4 channels) incurs a loss of approximately 3.5%, while the opacity-scaling cluster (4 channels) shows a loss of around 0.88%. A clear trend emerges when examining the spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients for color. The quantization losses for the 0th, 1st, 2nd, and 3rd order SH coefficients are 1.2%, 3.1%, 9.8%, and 17.9%, respectively.

7.4 Cross-platform viewer performance

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of our rendering system, we conducted a series of tests on both desktop and mobile platforms. The desktop testbed was a laptop equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 Laptop GPU and an Intel Core i7-11800H CPU, while the mobile devices included an M2-powered iPad Pro, an iPhone 15 Pro, and an Apple Vision Pro. Using a dynamic single-person sequence composed of 180,000 Gaussian splats as the test sample, our Unity-based renderer achieved a fluid playback speed of over 400 FPS on the desktop platform, with a memory footprint of approximately 900 MB and average CPU and GPU utilization of 30% and 57%, respectively. On mobile devices such as the iPad, iPhone, and Vision Pro, the peak rendering framerate for the native application stabilized at approximately 90 FPS for the iPad and iPhone, and 30 FPS for the Vision Pro. For the web-based renderer on desktop, the framerate was locked at 30 FPS with resource utilization comparable to the native Unity application, demonstrating cross-platform stability.

8 Discussion and future work

While our method achieves remarkable results in reconstructing appearance, it still faces fundamental challenges in acquiring high-precision, temporally consistent normals and surfaces. These challenges primarily stem from the coupling of complex non-rigid motion and occlusion under different viewpoints. Relying solely on single-frame or local geometric information for normal estimation tends to introduce noise and temporal inconsistency. This not only affects the visual quality of the reconstructed surface but also hinders downstream applications such as relighting and material editing. Future work may explore introducing temporal constraints—for instance, using optical flow or learned motion priors to smooth and regularize the evolution of the normal field—or designing specialized network architectures that jointly optimize geometry and normals. The goal is to achieve stable, physically plausible normal estimates while preserving fine details.

Our current research focuses on high-fidelity reconstruction in human-centric scenarios, where the background is treated as something to be removed. Such a representation allows flexible integration of the reconstructed subject into large-scale or virtual environments. However, since relighting and material editing techniques related to 3D Gaussian Splatting are not yet fully developed, compositing multiple scenes may still yield unrealistic results. Reconstructing scenes with backgrounds can produce complete and authentic environments. Extending the current method to such general scenarios faces several key issues: how to robustly segment foreground from background, how to handle complex occlusion and shadow interactions between dynamic objects and the background, and how to efficiently model the background without significantly increasing computational cost. A promising direction is to introduce a layered representation model. For example, decomposing the scene into a static background layer and a dynamic subject layer, and designing an efficient compositing rendering mechanism. Going further, exploring lightweight representations of dynamic backgrounds (such as swaying trees or flowing water) and studying their impact on overall model capacity and rendering performance will be critical steps toward truly general dynamic scene understanding and reconstruction. Alternatively, adopting an implicit representation to model 4D Gaussians and reducing the strong dependency on point cloud tracking is also a feasible idea.

Although 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has achieved real-time rendering efficiency, its training process still heavily relies on traditional stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimizers. The inherent linear convergence speed of SGD and its sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning remain bottlenecks to further improving training efficiency. This is especially evident with long sequences or high-resolution inputs, where training time remains considerable. We believe future research should fundamentally shift the optimization paradigm. One direction is algorithmic: exploring optimization methods with higher-order convergence properties. Although these may increase per-iteration cost, they could converge in much fewer iterations, thereby reducing total training time. Another direction is to move away from per-scene iterative optimization and instead design a feedforward generative network that takes multi-view video as input and directly predicts per-frame dynamic Gaussian attributes (position, rotation, scale, color, etc.) in an end-to-end manner. If such a highly generalizable feedforward paradigm capable of achieving quality comparable to optimization-based methods can be realized, it would reduce dynamic reconstruction from minutes or hours to seconds, opening new possibilities for real-time dynamic content creation, editing, and transmission. However, achieving this requires addressing challenges such as constructing large-scale high-quality dynamic datasets and improving the generalization ability of networks. Current attempts at feedforward 3DGS generation still struggle to match the quality of optimization-based results.

Our current representation essentially maintains an independent Gaussian point cloud for each frame. Compared to implicit representations, such as using networks to predict Gaussian attributes, this approach offers more flexibility in handling large motions. Moreover, it demonstrates a superior capacity for representing intricate fine-grained textural details. However, it also has clear drawbacks: it ignores the extensive geometric and appearance redundancy between frames, leading to storage and transmission costs that grow almost linearly with sequence length, making it difficult to apply to long sequences or resource-constrained scenarios. The key breakthrough in the future lies in how to intelligently establish and utilize cross-frame correspondences. One may explore embedding cross-frame sharing mechanisms. For example, establishing correspondences between Gaussians across frames, representing largely static or rigid parts as shared point sets, and dynamically allocating and updating only regions with motion or deformation. Another idea is to combine explicit and implicit representations to leverage the advantages of both, ensuring both flexibility for large motion and the use of temporal continuity. Furthermore, borrowing ideas from video encoding, such as using motion vectors to describe Gaussian trajectories rather than storing absolute positions per frame, could be promising. Such a highly structured representation would not only further improve the compression rate of dynamic sequences, enabling high-quality streaming of dynamic scenes at ultra-low bandwidth, but also inherently enhance temporal consistency. This would provide strong structural priors for downstream tasks such as motion analysis, temporal super-resolution, and controllable temporal interpolation and extrapolation.

9 Conclusion

Volumetric video is revolutionizing immersive media, with 3D Gaussian Splatting emerging as a key technology. The application of 3D Gaussian Splatting technology to dynamic scenes in volumetric video presents two significant limitations: massive storage and transmission overhead associated with temporal sequences, and a fragmented toolchain ecosystem that hinders efficient research and development. To address these limitations, a comprehensive pipeline for dynamic Gaussian reconstruction is presented in our study, encompassing the process from data processing to viewer playback. To ensure broad accessibility, we develop a cross-platform rendering solution that supports high-quality, interactive, free-viewpoint experiences on desktop, mobile, and XR devices. We also contribute a large-scale, human-centered action performance dataset. Our integrated framework and dataset aim to bridge the entire pipeline from data creation to end-user application, providing a solid foundation for the large-scale adoption and future research of Gaussian Splatting technology.
