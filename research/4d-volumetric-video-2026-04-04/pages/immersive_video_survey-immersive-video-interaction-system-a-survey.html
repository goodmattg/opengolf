Abstract

As communication technologies undergo rapid evolution, human interaction technologies have become increasingly efficient and accessible. Videoconferencing, for example, facilitates real-time, face-to-face communication worldwide, providing unprecedented convenience. Nevertheless, traditional two-dimensional (2D) videoconferencing systems frequently fall short of delivering a fully immersive experience, as they lack the ability to convey physical presence, depth perception, and natural interaction—key elements that are of particular significance in replicating the subtleties inherent in in-person communication. This disconnection leaves users yearning for the rich, multi-sensory experiences that real-life interactions can offer. In response, significant research efforts have been dedicated to the development of immersive video interaction systems (IVISs). This paper introduces the concept of IVISs and reviews a spectrum of systems advanced by researchers and industry innovators, each of whom contributes unique insights into the evolution of this field. A comparative analysis of the characteristics of current IVISs is provided, offering insights across multiple dimensions. This survey concludes with a discussion of potential future research directions and the challenges that remain in enhancing the IVIS technology. Through a comprehensive analysis, this work aims to foster further exploration and innovation in immersive communication technologies.

1 Introduction

The advent of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) has precipitated an unprecedented paradigm shift in global communication patterns, with online videoconferencing emerging as a preeminent medium for seamless global connections through devices equipped with webcams, microphones, and speakers. This paradigm shift has catalyzed significant advancements in video interaction technologies, thereby pushing the boundaries of how video communication is utilized. Historically, the development of stereoscopic telepresence systems has been an area of intense research, discussed as early as the last century [1–3]. Early endeavors in this field [4–8] sought to enhance the sense of co-presence by integrating three-dimensional (3D) models of participants into unified virtual scenarios. These systems allowed users to engage with and navigate through 3D environments captured via cameras, fostering a more immersive experience than two-dimensional (2D) displays. Despite initial limitations in providing superior quality due to hardware constraints in compression and rendering, these pioneering efforts ignited interests in immersive communication technologies. Subsequent research [9–14] has made substantial strides in improving real-time 3D model reconstruction and enhancing live streaming architectures. However, these systems still struggled with user experience issues and had limited application scope due to the reliance on extensive camera arrays, which also made them prohibitively expensive. Recent advancements in hardware, software, and contents’ production [15, 16] have led to revolutionary improvements in telepresence systems, dramatically expanding their capabilities in terms of communication distance, immersion, interactivity, real-time performance, and affordability. This progress represents a significant leap forward in facilitating connectivity and interaction across vast distances, facilitating the reshaping of remote communication.

To comprehensively understand current advancements in telepresence, this survey first defines IVIS as a modern and advanced telepresence system engineered to deliver a deeply immersive user experience. IVISs have an extensive range of application. In the education sector, teachers can use IVISs to remotely demonstrate 3D content, such as geometric models, building structures, and mechanical components, to students more intuitively. Additionally, for dangerous or impractical experiments in regular teaching settings, students can engage in absolutely safe and immersive operations within a simulated environment using IVISs. In the domain of telemedicine, IVISs can be employed to deploy a visual vital sign monitoring system, enabling healthcare professionals to access more intuitive visual representations in conjunction with cardiac, blood pressure, and respiratory data. This facilitates more efficient and accurate consultations. Combined with person re-identification technology [17–20], IVISs can perform cross-view matching and retrieval of specific pedestrians within a network of cameras, making it useful for intelligent surveillance and traffic management. Furthermore, IVISs have foreseeable application in areas such as archaeology, transportation, and aerospace. IVISs can vary widely in structure and composition. However, their basic components are inherited from traditional videoconferencing technologies. As depicted in Fig. 1, at its core, an IVIS includes modules for acquisition, codec (encoder and decoder), transmission, rendering, and display. This modular framework allows for flexible modifications and the integration of new components to enhance immersion from various viewing aspects. Several innovative IVIS examples illustrate this adaptability and the potential for enhanced user interaction. For instance, VirtualCube [21] organizes each participant within a standardized cube, facilitating the creation of a virtual mini-conference room atmosphere by supporting various cube combinations. RemoteTouch [22] employs screen vibrations to simulate physical touch among users, enhancing the sense of presence and connection. Meanwhile, Project Starline [23] pushes the boundaries of realism in video interactions with a display system that integrates multiple capture devices, providing an exceptionally lifelike and dynamic interaction experience. These novel modular designs not only enhance user experience but also pave the way for future innovations in the field of video interaction systems, setting new industry standards for immersive communication.

Both traditional videoconferencing system and IVISs are essentially part of the video communication field. The IVISs differ in several aspects from traditional videoconferencing. First, traditional videoconferencing prioritizes stability and efficiency, making communication possible on any devices under various network conditions. By contrast, IVIS focuses on pioneering practices in ideal environments to enhance users’ visual experience. In addition, IVISs could also allow for asymmetrical communication between parties. For example, the system proposed by Huang et al. [24] does not include a display device on the sender’s side, as its main purpose is to optimize the 3D visual experience on the receiver’s side. Moreover, IVISs are more scene-oriented than traditional videoconferencing. While videoconferencing only provides audio-visual interaction between participants, IVISs are designed to better integrate the scene with users and objects within it. Overall, IVISs demonstrate significant potential, which will be further explored with the advance of video acquisition technologies and computing devices, and gradually reveal the superiority in enabling next-generation immersive video communication.

As such, this survey aims to address the existing gap in the comprehensive understanding of IVISs by providing a structured overview. Starting with a definition of IVISs, the paper then delves into their development, existing contributions, and future prospects. The challenges these systems face are also outlined. The main contributions of this study are detailed as follows:

  1. 1)

    We introduce and define the concept of immersive video interaction systems (IVISs), highlighting their unique characteristics and differentiating them from traditional videoconferencing systems. This provides a systematic theoretical framework for understanding IVISs and paves the way for future developments in this area.

  2. 2)

    We conduct a comprehensive survey of existing IVISs, organizing and comparing their parameters and features to offer valuable insights for researchers and practitioners, thus laying a foundation for future studies and applications in the field.

  3. 3)

    We provide a forward-looking discussion on the future prospects of IVISs, outlining key challenges and potential research directions. This highlights the opportunities for further advancements in technology, user experience, and system integration, offering a roadmap for future exploration in the field.

2 Architecture

As shown in Fig. 1, a typical IVIS can be abstracted into several components: acquisition, codec (encoder and decoder), transmission, rendering, and display. For clarity, the following description of the IVIS structure will be based on a one-way process.

The acquisition module functions to capture the multi-dimensional information of the participants and scenes. This module often consists of stationary camera arrays, depth sensors, or multiple color (red, green, blue) and depth (RGBD) cameras distributed in the scene, simulating the viewer’s visual system in the target environment. This setup provides stereoscopic vision to the viewer, greatly enhancing the sense of immersion. For IVISs based on 3D data format (e.g., point cloud or mesh), the rich environmental information provided by depth maps plays a crucial role in reconstructing 3D models. The acquisition module also preprocesses the captured visual information to facilitate network transmission and reduce bandwidth usage.

Due to the multiple viewpoints and 3D scene information involved, an IVIS typically requires the transmission of a large amount of video data, which is usually directly correlated with the number of cameras and the resolution requirements. Without effective compression, the data could exceed the capacity of network transmission, leading to increased end-to-end latency and a degradation of the user experience. To address this, NVIDIA developed NVENC (NVIDIA encoder) and NVDEC (NVIDIA decoder) to provide hardware acceleration for video codecs across various formats (H.264 [25] and HEVC [26]). Meanwhile, intelligent video coding (IVC) [27] is continuously evolving and refined. These technologies dramatically reduce central processing unit (CPU) utilization and have been widely adopted in recent IVISs.

The encoded data need to be transmitted to the receiver through the network, and the transmission module must ensure reliability, low latency, and stability for the entire system to achieve real-time video interaction. Web real-time communication (WebRTC) [28] facilitates efficient, low-latency communication across diverse data types while ensuring cross-platform compatibility. Owing to these capabilities, WebRTC has become a prevalent choice among IVISs. In some cases, the preprocessing stage of the acquisition module achieves most of the optimizations for visual information, eliminating the need for a separate rendering module.

The decoded visual information needs to be rendered to reproduce the model, lighting, textures, and other elements for the user. Through rendering, complex scenes and interactions are dynamically transformed into vivid outputs, where high-quality rendering enhances realism and immersion. To achieve seamless operation in intricate scenes and provide high-quality visual effects despite limited hardware resources, IVISs usually deploy a range of optimization strategies [29, 30] during the rendering procedure.

The display module is responsible for presenting rendered images, 3D models, or other visual content to the user, serving as a visual bridge between the user and the remote scene. To enhance immersion, cutting-edge technologies such as high-resolution, low-latency, and high-refresh-rate displays can be utilized to make the scene more realistic. Most IVISs need to support stereoscopic vision (e.g., 3D displays) to provide the user with depth perception, which is essential for an immersive experience.

3 Immersive video interaction systems

This section introduces 17 existing IVISs, focusing on the associated motivation, structure design, technical details, and implementation. We also summarize the key attributes of these IVISs for comparison in Table 1.

3.1 General-purpose telepresence with head-worn optical see-through displays and projector-based lighting (2013)

Maimone et al. [31] mentioned the low contrast and transparency issues of projection-based remote characters on local see-through displays when without lighting control. In a normally-lit room, this can result in unrecognizable character projections. To this end, they designed a general-purpose telepresence system that utilizes optical see-through head-worn displays and projector-based lighting control to present the image of a remote participant. In the display module, a lighting control system is introduced. It generates a black mask on the local background for the remote character’s projection based on the occlusion relationship, which immersively conveys the correct perspective of the character with the local environment. In the prototype system, multiple Microsoft Kinect depth sensors are utilized to create a real-time 3D representation of the environment, merging remote and local scenes into a shared coordinate space. All equipment in both rooms is driven by a personal computer (PC) with dual NVIDIA GTX 580 graphics processing units (GPUs). Epson Moverio commercial off-the-shelf optical see-through glasses provide a clear and natural local view with immersive eye contact. The method proposed by Maimone et al. [31] optimized character projections and accurate object occlusion relationships in the experiments. Nonetheless, the figure images still have rough edges, and the high latency causes a “swimming” effect, leading to temporary misalignment between the figure and the black mask when the remote participant moves fast.

3.2 Holoportation (2016)

Holoportation [32] is a fully immersive 3D audio-visual interaction system developed by Microsoft Research. To enable users to interact immersively with a distant space (e.g., an entire room), Holoportation utilizes a set of depth cameras in its acquisition module to accurately reproduce all objects in a scene, including characters, furniture, and other common items. The rendering module employs a lightweight compression and rendering process, complemented by a dual-GPU reconstruction strategy, effectively reducing end-to-end communication latency. Additionally, the surrounding sound is captured and delivered through the headset, enhancing the immersive experience for the user. In the prototype system of Holoportation, each capture site is set up with four PCs with Intel Core i7 3.4 GHz CPUs, 16 GB of RAM, and two NVIDIA Titan X GPUs for depth computation and foreground segmentation. Depth maps and segmented color images are propagated to dedicated dual GPU devices. Eight surrounding camera pods allow the local user to walk freely throughout the scene and interact with remote users and objects. Unlike methods that focus on presenting individual portraits, Holoportation creates 3D simulations of all objects within a space. By wearing a virtual reality (VR) headset, users can view rendered 3D models of objects in the target space and observe real-time motions from all directions. However, since the reconstruction and rendering of 3D models must be performed in real-time, the displayed 3D models of people and objects lose some details and may appear distorted. Nonetheless, Holoportation still demonstrates excellent immersive performance and offers potentially rich application scenarios.

3.3 TeleHuman2 (2018)

To ensure that the telepresence system supports the richness of multiparty conversation, Gotsch et al. [33] designed a “holographic” telepresence system: TeleHuman2, which conveys motion parallax and stereoscopic sensations without the use of head-mounted devices. A depth camera is utilized in the acquisition module to eliminate the need for a separate camera for each projector, allowing for the adjustment of vertical parallax through a virtual camera, thereby simulating eye contact. For the rendering module, each projector utilizes a dedicated processor for parallel rendering, largely reducing time delays. TeleHuman2’s display module employs a cylindrical display, which is slightly taller than the human body, providing a full range of parallax. This setup supports multiple participants conversing with a single remote presenter while maintaining an appropriate stereoscopic experience. To address the degradation of resolution caused by lenslet array and parallax barrier schemes, Gotsch et al. [33] adopted a retro-reflective solution that provides full 720p spatial resolution through an array of miniature projectors. Another challenge is the reduction in overall display brightness caused by the parallax barrier, which is mitigated by using a focused retroreflector. TeleHuman’s angular resolution criterion uses the average interpupillary distance of 6.3 cm, with a circular array of two projectors separated by less than 6 cm. To achieve better motion parallax and stereo setup, the brightness of the retroreflector is adjusted downward by 1.3 degrees, ensuring that each eye of the viewer can see only one projected image at a time, the one closest to that eye. TeleHuman2’s prototype system utilizes a display made of a hollow acrylic tube that is 195 cm high, 75 cm in diameter, and 5 mm thick and coated with a reflective film. The projector array consists of three groups of 15 PicoPro micro laser projectors attached to a circular track in 3D-printed mounts. Each projector is connected to an Odroid C1+ board that acts as a rendering engine, achieving an average rendering speed of 10 fps. In the remote capture room, the speaker stands in a circle with a radius of 180 cm, with NVIDIA GTX 1080-equipped ZED cameras set up every 20 degrees. Within the capture room, stereo images are converted into depth maps and RGB images for presentation to the viewer. The viewer is shown a 180-degree cutout of the speaker captured from all directions by the Ricoh Theta S camera. Surround sound for TeleHuman2 is provided by five speakers arranged in a circular array around the projector. TeleHuman2 enables all individuals around the cylindrical display to move freely while viewing a life-sized participant from all angles, making it highly suitable for applications such as remote presentations. However, projector arrays raise the cost of the entire system, making it difficult to be widely adopted.

3.4 An immersive bidirectional system for life-size 3D communication (2018)

Plüss et al. [34] presented a bidirectional life-size 3D telecommunication system and developed a suite of supporting hardware and software devices. Users can communicate immersively over distances of thousands of kilometers by wearing passive polarized glasses. The acquisition device consists of a pair of high-resolution color cameras and a pair of Kinect depth cameras. The cameras are mounted on the left and right sides of a 3D display at the same height as a person, and this integrated design facilitates bidirectional communication. For the acquisition module, since a full-body image of the participant must be displayed on the transparent screen while excluding the background, image segmentation is necessary. The researcher simplified the segmentation process by assuming a known background and employed frame differencing or chroma key-based segmentation to improve efficiency. To enable ultra-long-distance communication, the transmission module utilizes stereo pair-based transmission. Two system setups were established in Zurich and Singapore to evaluate the frame rate and latency of long-distance transmission. For the rendering module, point-based rendering is not utilized due to its significant computational overhead. Instead, the surface is rendered as an individual triangle mesh for each depth camera. The entire system is highly adaptable to various scenarios, including both 3D-3D and 3D-2D configurations. For instance, one end can be replaced with a 2D display, or the capture device can be substituted with a 2D webcam. The experiments were conducted at a bandwidth of 0.35 Mbps and a resolution of 1080×960, revealing a frame rate of 20 fps and a latency of 1.4 s. Standard desktop computers (Intel Core i7-2600K 3.40 GHz, 16 GB RAM, NVIDIA GTX 680 GPU) are used on both the acquisition and display sides. Similar to some previous IVISs, the method proposed by Plüss et al. [34] is also primarily designed for life-sized remote presentations. However, this approach supports both 2D and 3D capture and display. Additionally, due to its asymmetrical design, the system is demonstrated to be effective in one-to-many scenarios, making it suitable for teaching or demonstration purposes. A transparent display screen has been employed, raising concerns about the overall system’s performance under excessive ambient lighting.

3.5 FVV live (2020)

As an end-to-end free viewpoint video (FVV) system, FVV Live [35], which can be built with off-the-shelf hardware components, offers the advantages of low cost and real-time availability. The acquisition module of FVV Live generates a data format containing texture and geometry information from multiple viewpoints, enabling the system to render the scene from any perspective. Passive stereo cameras (Stereolab ZED) are employed to avoid interference between active devices. The acquired depth data are processed by a depth correction block and a background (BG) depth removal block. To address the challenges posed by large data volumes, FVV Live’s compression and transmission module adopts a strategy that encodes and transmits color and depth data streams independently. Lossy H.264/AVC encoders are used for the color data stream, while lossless compression is applied to the depth data stream to preserve the quality of the views that are synthesized. For the rendering module, the synthesis of viewpoints is based on the depth-image-based rendering (DIBR) paradigm [45], which addresses the problems of crack artifacts and occlusions through various existing optimization methods [46–48]. Each capture server in the FVV Live prototype is equipped with an NVIDIA GTX 1080 GPU and an NVIDIA Quadro P4000, enabling efficient depth calculation and video encoding across multiple cameras. The ZED cameras operate at a 1080p resolution with a frame rate of 30 fps. The Edge server is equipped with an NVIDIA GTX 1080 GPU, which manages the decoding of all incoming multiview plus depth (MVD) streams and facilitates virtual view synthesis. FVV Live boasts an end-to-end latency of 252 ms, making it suitable for immersive videoconferencing. Subjective experiments indicate that FVV Live performs optimally in uncomplicated scenarios and demonstrates fair to good performance in medium to complex scenarios. Unlike previous IVISs, FVV Live supports mobile devices as output, allowing users to conduct real-time 3D exploration of an entire space on their smartphones. This development signifies an endeavor towards the creation of a lightweight IVIS system.

3.6 VirtualCube (2021)

Zhang et al. [21] discovered that traditional videoconferencing impedes participants from making effective eye contact and hinders their ability to turn their heads and communicate with those positioned to the side. To address these limitations, they proposed a 3D conferencing system called VirtualCube. This system virtually replicates a real-world cubicle, aiming to enhance the overall immersiveness in the communication experience. The novel standardized design enables VirtualCube to support a wide variety of combinations, thereby creating the illusion that participants are physically present in an office or conference room. Each VirtualCube unit is designed with simplicity and functionality in mind. The unit features a rectangular room layout, with a fixed-position seat centrally located and a table positioned against the front wall. The front and side walls are equipped with large screens to enhance the immersive experience. Mounted above the front screen is a set of RGBD cameras belonging to the acquisition module, which capture both the geometry and texture of the participant. These cameras record at a resolution of 2560×1440 for RGB frames and 640×576 for depth frames, operating at 30 frames per second. For high-quality rendering on multiple large screens in real-time, Zhang et al. [21] applied the V-Cube View algorithm in the rendering module, which uses depth images to alleviate the burden of virtual view prediction and reduce resolution in computationally intensive processes. For the display module, each participant benefits from the front and side screens, which provide a wide field of view and display life-size images of others at natural angles. This setup immerses participants in the experience, simulating a meeting around a table regardless of the number of attendees. Using an Intel Core i9-10980XE CPU, two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs (for inputting and rendering RGBD images), and one NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU (for displaying the UI and rendering results), the system’s latency totaled about 300 ms, with frame rates of 23 fps in 3-person sessions and 30 fps in 2-person sessions, respectively. The newly proposed V-Cube View algorithm performs well across four subjects and three metrics: peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of generated images, mean square error (MSE) of alpha mapping, and perceptual error of synthesized faces. VirtualCube is the first to introduce a connectable modular design, which offers versatility across various scenarios. It serves as an innovative solution for roundtable and side-by-side meetings, and its unique standardized modules may also inspire future developments.

3.7 A multi-user oriented live free viewpoint video streaming system based on view interpolation (2022)

The computation of virtual view synthesis algorithms in FVV systems can pose a significant challenge to the entire system’s real-time performance. To address this, Hu et al. [36] introduced a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based interpolation algorithm for synthesizing dense virtual views in their FVV system, incorporating a multi-user streaming-oriented strategy. In their system, 12 cameras are arranged in an arc around the scene, with the angle of each camera adjusting as the scene changes. The acquisition module employs hardware-synchronized cameras to capture video from multiple viewpoints at 4K resolution and 60 fps. Depth maps are omitted due to the high cost and limitations in resolution and distance range of multiple depth cameras. In the transmission module, content delivery network (CDN) edge servers process incoming video streams and serve multiple clients simultaneously. For synthesizing viewpoints between two cameras, an optical flow-based view interpolation network called VINet is utilized in the rendering module. The prototype utilizes a collector cluster that includes 1K Panasonic LUMIX BGH1 cameras, four capture cards, and four capture servers. Media edge servers are equipped with four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs, each encoding video from three cameras. Most computationally intensive modules run on these GPUs. The client setup includes a computer and a cell phone. The PC client features an Intel Core i9 CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 3070 GPU for video decoding, while the cell phone is a Samsung Galaxy S21. This IVIS offers various real-time FVV presentation clients, including mobile devices, similar to FFV Live. The difference is that it does not utilize depth cameras, resulting in lower costs and a more lightweight design.

3.8 HoloKinect (2022)

Siemonsma and Bell [37] found that despite the maturity of 3D capture and display technologies, there are still relatively few applications for 3D telecommunication compared to traditional 2D videoconferencing. To this end, HoloKinect has been proposed as an open-source, user-friendly, and GPU-accelerated platform that enables bidirectional 3D videoconferencing on common commercial hardware. The HoloKinect pipeline is built with simplicity and effectiveness. The acquisition module utilizes the Microsoft Azure Kinect to capture color, depth, and audio data, and to preprocess both the depth and color maps. Initially, the depth map with a resolution of 640×576 is downsampled to 320×288 to reduce resolution and sensor noise. This downsampling also aids in compressing the depth image data by eliminating high-frequency information. For efficient online transmission using existing hardware and software, HoloKinect’s transmission module employs an image-based encoding method. The multiwavelength depth (MWD) encoded image data is transmitted as a 30-fps composite image stream over a standard Internet connection to a remote receiver. The rendering module reconstructs the 3D scene from the depth image. Upon reception, each composite video frame is processed by a custom GPU-accelerated compute shader in Unity, which decodes the MWD information to retrieve the depth and color data. In the display module, the system reconstructs the 3D mesh data, containing the 3D coordinates, which is then transmitted via HDMI to the Looking Glass Portrait multiscopic display. The Looking Glass Portrait is a picture frame-sized holographic 3D display that allows users to view it from any angle within a 58-degree viewing cone. The stereoscopic effect is so realistic that the display appears as a 3D box with depth. This process is facilitated by a series of existing plugins. As an open-source IVIS, HoloKinect is highly extensible and can be easily adapted for applications beyond bidirectional 3D video interaction.

3.9 LiveRecon3D (2022)

LiveRecon3D, proposed by Zhou et al. [38], is a cost-effective, real-time 3D video reconstruction system characterized by its high scalability. The system boasts a compact design, utilizing a single computer connected to multiple RGBD cameras to achieve real-time reconstruction and rendering of dynamic scenes. They employed Kinect v2 cameras in LiveRecon3D to capture and construct a multi-view dataset from three fixed positions. The system is capable of processing multiple synchronized RGBD video streams as input, as well as loading and playing 3D models from a local dataset. In the acquisition module, three Kinect v2 cameras are strategically positioned around the target, each operating on a dedicated thread to maintain a frame rate of 30 fps. To optimize computational resources, a background fading module is implemented, reducing the need to render static backgrounds continuously. The rendering module of LiveRecon3D applies an innovative adaptive volume integration strategy. This method consolidates depth maps from all camera views into a single volume, dynamically updating its position and size with each frame. It automatically tracks the reconstructed area and adjusts the resolution adaptively, ensuring efficient and precise 3D reconstruction. The entire system achieves full real-time performance at 30fps on a computer equipped with an Intel Core i9-K0900K CPU, 32 GB of RAM, and two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. LiveRecon3D enables the seamless integration of captured character models with virtual environments. While the reconstructed 3D models may display noticeable edge noise and voids, potentially affecting the user experience in subsequent applications, the system’s lightweight design and cost-effectiveness offer a significant trade-off for these quality limitations.

3.10 RemoteTouch (2023)

Zhang et al. [39] argued that touch, alongside vision, is a vital element of the immersive experience. To this end, they developed RemoteTouch, a screen vibration-based IVIS. RemoteTouch enhances immersion for both users by enabling “remote hand clapping.” The primary challenge in this design arises when the hand approaches to the screen, causing the cameras positioned at the display’s edges to lose the necessary angle to capture the RGBD data required for rendering the palm. To overcome this issue, Zhang et al. [39] introduced a dual representation of the hand, achieving realistic and seamless rendering through the mutual augmentation of images and 3D geometric models. In RemoteTouch, each user is equipped with a large display with a set of RGBD cameras mounted around it to capture the user’s geometry. A camera dedicated to tracking the hand is fitted on the side of the display. The remote hand touch interaction is divided into three phases: the user raises their hand (starting phase), the user moves their hand towards the display (approaching phase), and the palm touches the screen, completing the triggering of the vibration feedback (ending phase). The dual hand representation is based on both image and 3D geometry (from a rigged 3D mesh template) data. Images are used for rendering when the hand can still be fully captured, such as in the initial phase, while 3D geometry is employed when the hand can no longer be captured, such as in the final phase. For the intermediate phase, Zhang et al. [39] developed a distance-based fusion algorithm to ensure smooth transitions. RemoteTouch features two configured prototypes, each equipped with an Intel Core i9-10980XE CPU, 64 GB of RAM, and two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. The system’s total end-to-end latency over a 1 Gbps bandwidth is approximately 400 ms. Twenty-six participants were invited to experience RemoteTouch, with 92% reporting that the touch feature significantly enhanced their sense of immersion. Unlike previous IVISs, RemoteTouch does not focus on optimizing the original five modules of visual interaction. Instead, it pioneers a new approach by introducing haptic interaction alongside visual interaction. This undoubtedly provides users with a new immersive experience. However, from a practical perspective, it is important to consider that most displays are not currently optimized for touch services. The secretion of oil and sweat from the human palm raises concerns about whether frequent contact could affect the display, which may potentially impact visual interaction.

3.11 MetaStream (2023)

Guan et al. [40] believed that live volumetric video streaming has great potential in remote video interaction. Consequently, they proposed an immersive application based on real-time volumetric content called MetaStream. In the acquisition module, dynamic camera calibration (DCC) is applied to address the limitations of fixed camera adaptation to the scene. MetaStream uses a two-step workflow to create a point cloud of a target object: single-camera point cloud construction and multi-camera point cloud synthesis. This approach allows for the parallelization of point cloud construction across different cameras, effectively addressing the issues of asynchronous frame arrival and high computational cost. Given the limited computational resources of mixed reality headsets, the authors suggest leveraging foveated rendering in MetaStream to reduce overhead. The hardware for MetaStream’s prototype includes an AMD 5950X CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 2080S GPU, with all cameras being Intel RealSense L515s mounted on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano. Test results indicate that MetaStream can achieve frame rates close to 30 fps in WiFi network environments. Compared to previous state-of-the-art systems, MetaStream reduces end-to-end latency by 31.7% while improving visual quality by 12.5%. As the first real-time volumetric content capture, creation, delivery, and rendering system, MetaStream addresses the challenges of transmission delay, preprocessing overhead, point cloud rendering, and real-time tracking without compromising overall performance.

3.12 AI-mediated 3D video conferencing (2023)

In August 2023, NVIDIA proposed an artificial intelligence (AI)-mediated 3D videoconferencing system [41] that captures, reconstructs, and displays life-size 3D avatars using low-cost hardware devices. A novel 3D lifting method has been applied to the 3D capture process of the acquisition module, enabling the encoding of 2D inputs into efficient tri-planar neural representations. This system accommodates both realistic and stylized avatars while enabling eye contact. The sender requires only an RGB webcam for capture and transmission, while the receiver has the capability to convert the 2D video into an implicit tri-planar 3D representation by utilizing an encoder based on a vision transformer. To enable view tracking, gaze information redirected by neural algorithms is employed to correct the gaze in 2D images. These 2D images are subsequently transformed into 3D and optimized for real-time viewing on existing 3D displays. This system facilitates immersive 3D video interaction using only consumer-grade devices, with AI-based lifting methods being integral to its functionality.

3.13 Live4D (2023)

Noting that the use of volumetric video in virtual and augmented reality is limited by expensive hardware and the difficulty of processing the data, Zhou et al. [42] proposed Live4D, a cost-effective streaming system based on volumetric video. The acquisition module of Live4D consists of two key components: the front end and the reconstruction server. The front end includes multiple binocular industrial cameras and a computation station, which generates multi-view synchronized RGBD frames. The reconstruction server then receives these RGBD frames and performs further reconstruction. Since deep learning-based methods have limitations in reconstructing depth information, Live4D uses an algorithm that combines TensorRT and CUDA. Furthermore, they implemented a region-of-interest strategy that identifies and crops significant areas from the input image for individual processing. This approach enhances disparity accuracy in critical regions, such as faces. To minimize costs, Live4D generates efficient networks by reusing the truncated signed distance function (TSDF) [49] in conjunction with implicit neural SDF. During the post-processing stage, seamless texture maps are created using a weighted blending technique that leverages the distance field within the imaging space. Additionally, a half-edge mesh structure is employed to iteratively propagate color across untextured surfaces, effectively addressing the issue of missing textures and ensuring a more complete and coherent texturing result. Experimental results demonstrate that Live4D achieves a performance of 24 fps when utilizing an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, with an end-to-end latency of 95 ms, underscoring its efficiency and responsiveness. Unlike previous methods, the majority of algorithms in Live4D are used to optimize the acquisition module, aiming to capture more realistic 3D models while minimizing network transmission pressure.

3.14 Tele-Aloha (2024)

To provide the best possible quality immersive experience for consumer-level users, Tu et al. [43] proposed Tele-Aloha, a low-cost, high-fidelity telepresence system. Tele-Aloha’s acquisition module eliminates the need for expensive and light-sensitive optical sensors, consisting of only four RGB cameras positioned on the left, right, and top of the display. A well-designed cascade disparity estimator is used to acquire the geometry of the target, thus solving the disparity estimation problem for wide-baseline stereo cameras. Timestamp insertion synchronizes the 16-bit, 48,000 Hz two-channel audio with the video stream. Additionally, Tele-Aloha utilizes an innovative visual synthesis technique to generate realistic upper-body views in real-time. A cascaded multi-view capture system, comprising four cameras divided into upper and lower pairs, stabilizes the synthesis of new views. For the rendering module, the extracted pixel alignment features from the input image are projected onto the selected new view using a generic 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterizer [50], ensuring the integrity of the rendering process. In their prototype, the camera boasts a resolution of 4096×3000 pixels and operates at a refresh rate of 30 Hz. The captured data are encoded into an HEVC video stream and transmitted using low-latency WebRTC technology. Coding is handled by an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, utilizing the NVENC/NCDEC codec. Images from the four cameras are cropped into squares, stitched together into a single 6000×6000 resolution image, and then fed into NVENC for encoding. The upper pair’s inspections serve as an initialization to more accurately estimate the disparity of the lower pair. Following this, the image undergoes 3D Gaussian Splicing-based neural rendering and is then processed by a lightweight refinement module to produce the final output at full resolution. The entire system is implemented on CUDA, achieving a final latency of 23.5 ms. Tele-Aloha differs from previous IVISs in that it places greater emphasis on capturing the appearance details of participants. It achieves an immersive experience through carefully designed simple configurations. Depth sensors are not used due to cost considerations, and all equipment consists of readily available commercial products, rendering it a promising candidate for mass-produced consumer-grade IVIS.

3.15 Project starline (2024)

Project Starline [23] is an immersive, real-time bidirectional communication system developed by Google Research, allowing people who are far apart to converse as if they were face-to-face. Project Starline is committed to achieving breakthroughs in the following dimensions: 3D audiovisual likeness, comfortable display, and copresence. The authors highlight the challenges of attempting to outperform 2D videoconferencing with 3D telepresence, specifically the inherently high fidelity of 2D video and the trade-offs in quality that must be made with existing stereoscopic technologies. To achieve seamless, high-fidelity symmetric telepresence with a strong sense of copresence, a display unit is equipped with a comprehensive set of multifunctional modules. These modules integrate acquisition, tracking, rendering, and audiovisualization capabilities. The unit features six stereo cameras, four tracking cameras, four color cameras, four infrared (IR) projectors, and two loudspeakers, all mounted on an 8K LCD screen. In the acquisition module, a new capture pod has been developed for the capture module, as commercially available RGBD sensors fall short of the required reconstruction quality. Each pod is equipped with a 1600×1200 RGB camera for texture measurement and a 1280×1024 near-infrared (NIR) monochrome camera. Four scene image pairs, illuminated with NIR point patterns from various angles, along with a guide image pair, collectively generate a 60-Hz depth map. The two top pods on the left and right capture the torso of a seated user, while a specialized pod at the bottom includes a second RGB camera with a shorter focal length to capture detailed facial features. Four synchronized 1280×1024 monochrome cameras ensure precise 3D tracking. Thirty-four landmarks are set for facial recognition, with the 2D positions of facial features weighted based on the proximity to these landmarks. For each captured image, all landmarks are detected and used for tracking purposes. For the codec, four NVENC/NVDEC units with NVIDIA GPUs handle the HEVC codec for depth images. The quantization parameter (QP) for depth data is set to 14, while for color data, it is set to 22. For the rendering module, the decompressed depth images are locally rendered based on the positions of the left and right eyes separately. The system incorporates a fast rasterization scheme and an image-based fusion algorithm as an assisted rendering strategy. In addition to the display unit, there is a backlight unit designed as a bench that houses the IR backlight. White LEDs are installed on the sides and back of the display and backlight units, creating a soft light source that is diffusely reflected by the surrounding walls. This design minimizes highlights caused by direct illumination, thereby optimizing the rendering process while ensuring user comfort. Additionally, Project Starline ingeniously combines multiple sound processing techniques to create a sense of shared spatial naturalness without the need for headphones. Project Starline has garnered high praise from users following a small-scale deployment, with 87% of participants in 296 surveys rating the system as a superior experience compared to traditional videoconferencing. Its integrated design is more mature compared to many previous prototypes, providing a solid foundation for future mass production. However, the high cost of the hardware makes it difficult for average consumers to afford such a set of IVISs.

3.16 Learning view synthesis for desktop telepresence with few RGBD cameras (2024)

Wang et al. [44] found that it is challenging for recent telepresence systems to be both low-cost and high-quality simultaneously. Therefore, they proposed a high-quality view synthesis method with the goal of commercializing immersive telepresence systems. To maintain high-fidelity view synthesis while using only a small number of commercially available cameras, the acquisition module uses RGBD cameras to obtain additional geometrical information about the scene and reduce ambiguity in feature matching. In the rendering module, a feed-forward neural renderer that does not require per-video optimization is designed to enable efficient and versatile view compositing for desktop systems with large baselines and few cameras. Meanwhile, to address the nonlinear bias exhibited by depth measurements, they designed multi-layer point cloud (MPC), a novel volumetric representation aimed at removing bias and improving reconstruction quality. In addition, spatial skip connection (SSC) is proposed to optimize the upsampling of images on limited video memory. In the prototype system, four Microsoft Azure Kinect RGBD cameras are placed on the left, top, and right sides of a 23-inch display to provide complete portrait coverage. The neural renderer utilizes an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. In this work, the high-fidelity novel views are primarily generated by the proposed neural renderer. Since it only uses a few RGBD cameras, the complete system enables user-friendly immersive 3D telepresence in office scenarios at a total cost of about $3100, making it more affordable compared to other systems.

3.17 Low-complexity 3D-vision conferencing system based on accelerated RIFE model (2024)

Huang et al. [24] proposed an immersive conferencing system that delivers smooth, high-fidelity, life-size remote stereoscopic displays. The acquisition module utilizes nine industrial cameras mounted on a long slide for capturing images, along with a signal generator that produces a stable transistor-transistor logic (TTL) square wave for synchronization. Eight of the cameras are responsible for capturing images, while one is used to provide the standard reference image plane. Eight images are captured at 40 fps, which are then processed and stitched together to produce a 3840×4320 frame. This stitched frame is encoded using the HEVC standard and NVENC hardware before being transmitted to the RTC server via a specialized transmission tool. At the receiver end, the frame is decoded into RGB format. The rendering module employs a real-time intermediate flow estimation (RIFE) model, IFNet [51], for binocular viewpoint synthesis. Two 1080p views representing the left and right eyes are synthesized with virtual point-of-view positions, ready for display on a 4K stereoscopic display. In the display module, two cameras positioned at the top of the screen of the receiver side track the user’s eye position in real-time, under the assumption that the gaze does not move vertically. The prototype system is powered by an Intel Core i9-13900KF CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, achieving an end-to-end latency of approximately 200 ms.

Compared to the traditional “depth fusion-color blending-rendering” process used in previous IVISs for stereoscopic vision generation, this system employs direct cross-view calculation, significantly reducing overall complexity. At the same time, the system innovatively uses RIFE-based accelerated view synthesis to generate binocular vision, which effectively helps to eliminate artifacts.

4 Existing challenges

4.1 Adaptation

While IVIS offers users enhanced immersive experiences compared to traditional 2D videoconferencing, it still faces significant challenges in terms of adaptation. First, as shown in Table 1, most recent IVISs cannot support bidirectional interactions, even though they could provide a satisfactory user experience. This limitation restricts its applicability to scenarios, such as lectures and presentations, as opposed to interactive videoconferencing. Besides, IVISs typically require the processing of large volumes of RGB and depth data to capture a full range of participants and scenarios. When used for remote interactions, the data volume imposes extremely high bandwidth demands. Additionally, the reconstruction and rendering of 3D models place a substantial burden on the client’s hardware. Meanwhile, it can be noted that most existing IVISs have complex structures and occupy considerable space. Consequently, lightweight application scenarios warrant careful consideration. IVISs have been slow to advance on mobile devices, and the most relevant application for mobile devices within current IVISs is real-time viewing of FVV on the client side [35, 36]. This real-time viewing necessitates a fully pre-assembled pipeline for acquisition, transmission, and rendering. In contrast to traditional conferencing, which can be deployed anytime and anywhere, the applicability of IVISs remains relatively constrained. However, as demonstrated by these works, while achieving immersive bidirectional communication is challenging, current mobile devices have proven to be entirely capable of handling the display tasks of IVISs. Using smartphones as 2D display terminals proves beneficial for remote, comprehensive previews of 3D static objects. For artifacts that are difficult to exhibit due to security and other considerations, multiple capture devices can be arranged around the object, and the rendered images from various angles can be uploaded to the cloud, creating an electronic museum exhibition. This allows users to view the high-definition models of the artifacts on their smartphones. With the progression of technology, increasingly powerful mobile devices are expected to emerge. More advanced mobile devices may enable symmetrical immersive communication among users. These devices will not only possess a substantial user base but also come with inherent advantages in terms of integration. Developing immersive video functionalities based on mobile device hardware has the potential to become the mainstream direction for future advancements.

4.2 Cost-effectiveness

Currently, IVISs face the challenge of balancing cost with user experience. Some IVISs [37, 38, 41, 43] prioritize cost-efficiency by developing more effective deep learning-based segmentation, rendering, and fusion modules. These advancements aim to deliver satisfactory results using fewer devices. Built on consumer-grade hardware, these IVISs offer high extensibility, allowing users to personalize system settings to suit their preferences. However, the trade-off often includes subpar visual effects and immersion, characterized by low 3D model realism, rough edges, and difficulties in seamlessly blending with the scene. Conversely, other IVISs, such as Project Starline [23], are designed with the goal of achieving the highest level of immersion. These systems typically feature an integrated design, incorporating multiple cameras and sensors arranged around a large display to ensure high-quality, omnidirectional data acquisition. The use of state-of-the-art devices in these systems results in exceptional display quality and significant commercialization potential. Nonetheless, the high cost of these advanced IVISs makes them less accessible to the average consumer. To address this, future IVIS development could focus on hybrid solutions that integrate modular hardware and adaptive software frameworks. By optimizing resource allocation based on user needs and application contexts, it may be possible to create systems that retain high levels of immersion without over-relying on costly equipment. Advances in AI-driven rendering and real-time data compression could also reduce hardware dependency, making IVISs more accessible and practical across various sectors, from education to remote collaboration. Such a balanced approach would contribute to a more scalable and cost-effective model for IVISs, expanding their reach and usability in everyday applications.

4.3 Standardization

As mentioned in Sect. 1, IVISs are highly diverse. Such a field currently lacks harmonized technical standards and protocols, leading to poor interoperability among different systems and devices. Various manufacturers adopt different technical solutions and architectures, leading to compatibility issues across devices and platforms, and increasing the complexity of development and deployment. Notably, some IVISs (Plüss et al. [34], VirtualCube [21], Project Starline [23]) have made significant efforts to standardize their practices, indicating a promising potential for future development. The method proposed by Plüss et al. [34] and Project Starline [23] both employ an integrated design, which provides them with a natural advantage in mass production. For VirtualCube, each individual unit is standardized, consisting of identical equipment and shaped as a uniformly sized rectangle. This design supports a wide variety of splicing methods and significantly simplifies the bulk construction of the cubes. We believe that for large-scale industrial deployment, an IVIS should adopt an integrated design, merging the acquisition and display modules. Since IVISs primarily rely on user-provided network environments, rendering capabilities, computational resources, and codec devices, a supporting driver is essential to effectively connect the various modules. Any redundant functions should be eliminated to maximize the user experience. In addition, the complexity of the structure is a primary reason for the low compatibility of IVISs. In future IVIS development, researchers should exercise caution in adopting large structures while optimizing the input and output interfaces of each module.

5 Vision-language models’ role on future IVISs

A vision-language model (VLM) is a multimodal model that learns from large volumes of image and text data, and VLMs have developed rapidly over the past two years. To the best of our knowledge, no mature IVIS has yet applied VLMs. Nevertheless, we believe that VLMs have the potential to play a pivotal role in the future development of IVIS. From a technical perspective of some IVISs, the participant’s portrait needs to be separated from the background. VLM, with its advanced image segmentation capabilities developed through extensive training in visual data, is well-suited to meet this requirement. For applications, VLM-based IVISs enable users to interact with IVISs through natural language commands, making the system more accessible and user-friendly. Users can engage in intuitive interactions simply by describing actions or asking questions about visual content without complex controls, gestures, or specialized training. Besides, VLMs could allow IVISs to better understand scenes by recognizing objects, contexts, and relationships within the visual environment. For example, in an educational IVIS setting, VLMs could help identify objects or actions and provide real-time, descriptive feedback or instructional overlays, making interactions more dynamic and informative. In global collaboration contexts, VLMs could convert written instructions into visual overlays, bridging language barriers and enhancing cross-cultural communication in immersive environments. This capability could be particularly valuable in collaborative or training scenarios that involve participants from diverse linguistic backgrounds. However, VLMs currently rely heavily on powerful computing equipment and robust network infrastructure, which poses a significant challenge to the seamless integration with IVISs, due to the technological constraints that currently exist. While the potential of VLMs in enhancing IVIS capabilities is promising, further advancements are needed before this integration can be fully realized. As computational efficiency and network capabilities continue to improve, a future can be anticipated in which VLMs and IVISs will be more effectively combined to unlock new possibilities in immersive interaction and user experience.

6 Conclusion

The advancements in the IVIS technology have engendered new avenues for interactive experiences, thus surpassing the limitations of traditional 2D video communication. This paper presents a comprehensive review of IVIS development. We begin by introducing the concept and definition of an IVIS, followed by an overview of its general architecture. Subsequently, we examine 17 typical IVISs, with a focus on their unique technical features, functionalities, and practical applications. Despite significant progress in IVIS technology, the implementation of the technology remains constrained by several challenges. High resource demands, a lack of standardized protocols, and limited interoperability between systems are notable barriers to the practical application of IVIS technology. Future research should prioritize the development of standardized frameworks, the reduction of system complexity, and the enhancement of both the performance and accessibility of the IVIS to make it more practical for common use. The integration of emerging technologies, such as VLMs, has emerged as a significant catalyst for innovation. VLMs could enable more intuitive, multimodal interactions within IVISs, allowing users to interact with and explore complex 3D environments through natural language commands and context-aware visual aids. By addressing these challenges and exploring new possibilities, the IVIS has the potential to transform remote interactions and provide highly immersive experiences in fields such as education, telemedicine, and cross-cultural collaboration.
